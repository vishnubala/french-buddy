# French Buddy — Curriculum Specification
### Parisian (Metropolitan) French · 12 weeks · A1 → strong A2

---

## 0. Honest scope statement (read this first)

This program takes a motivated adult beginner from **zero to a solid A2 / A2+** in 12 weeks at roughly **20 minutes per day plus real listening/speaking practice**. It does **not** reach B1 in that time — genuine A1→B1 is commonly estimated at 350–500 guided hours, and 84 × ~20 min ≈ 28 hours. Anyone promising "B1 in 12 weeks" from zero is selling the brochure, not the language. What 12 weeks *can* do is give you a dependable A2 spine (present, near-future, and past tenses; ~800–1,000 high-frequency words; the pragmatics to not sound rude in Paris) that immersion then converts into B1.

**Target variety:** Metropolitan / Parisian French. Standard register with explicit `tu` vs `vous` training, Parisian pronunciation, and France-specific number system (`soixante-dix`, `quatre-vingt-dix` — *not* Belgian/Swiss `septante`/`nonante`).

---

## 1. Design principles

1. **Function before grammar.** Each week is organized around what you can *do* (order a coffee, give directions, tell someone about your weekend), with grammar introduced as the machinery that function needs — not as a syllabus of rules for their own sake.
2. **High-frequency first.** The first 500 words cover a disproportionate share of everyday speech. We front-load them and delay the long tail.
3. **Pronunciation from day one.** French's beginner wall is phonological (nasal vowels, the uvular R, silent final consonants, liaison, `u [y]` vs `ou [u]`). If you postpone it, you fossilize an anglophone accent. So it's Day 1, then reinforced weekly.
4. **Register is content, not a footnote.** `tu`/`vous` and the *bonjour* ritual are taught as core survival skills. Getting them wrong is the fastest way to read as rude in France.
5. **Spaced retrieval.** Vocabulary and structures resurface on an expanding schedule (see §5), not once-and-gone.
6. **Comprehensible input + output.** Every lesson has listening (native-style audio) and a low-stakes production step. Passive recognition alone doesn't build speech.

---

## 2. Lesson anatomy (the fixed template every day follows)

Each of the 84 lessons is one **data object** consumed by a single rendering engine. The steps:

| Step | Type key | Purpose |
|------|----------|---------|
| 1 | `intro` | 2–3 lines: today's function + why it matters |
| 2 | `phonics` | one sound/prosody focus, with contrast pairs |
| 3 | `vocab` | 6–10 items, each with audio, gender, register note |
| 4 | `dialogue` | a short native-style exchange (listening) |
| 5 | `recall` | retrieval quiz (MCQ / type-answer) feeding mastery score |
| 6 | `culture` | one Parisian pragmatics or cultural note |
| 7 | `wrap` | streak, mastery tally, preview of tomorrow |

One review/lighter day per week (usually day 7) swaps `phonics`+`vocab` for a consolidation `recall` over the week's items.

## 3. The 12-week arc

### Block A — Foundations (Weeks 1–2)

**Week 1 · Sounds & survival**
Function: greet, introduce yourself, be polite, count to 20.
Grammar: `être`; subject pronouns (`je/tu/il/elle/on`); `c'est`; gender of nouns (introduced); definite articles `le/la/les`.
Phonics: nasal vowels `on/an/in`; the French `u [y]`; silent final consonants.
Vocab spine: bonjour, salut, bonsoir, merci, s'il vous plaît, au revoir, oui/non, je m'appelle, enchanté(e), 0–20.
Paris note: the **bonjour ritual** (greet before you ask); `tu` vs `vous` first pass.

**Week 2 · People & identity**
Function: say where you're from, what you do, describe people simply.
Grammar: `avoir`; regular `-er` verbs (`parler, habiter, travailler`); negation `ne… pas`; `il y a`; question with `est-ce que`.
Phonics: the uvular `R`; liaison (first pass).
Vocab: nationalities, professions, family basics, 20–69.
Paris note: `on` used for "we" in everyday speech.

### Block B — Getting around (Weeks 3–5)

**Week 3 · Café & eating out**
Function: order, ask the price, pay.
Grammar: `je voudrais`; partitive `du/de la/des`; `un/une`; `combien`.
Phonics: `é [e]` vs `è [ɛ]`; the mute `e`.
Vocab: café/brasserie items, 70–100 (**soixante-dix, quatre-vingts, quatre-vingt-dix** — the France-specific forms).
Paris note: `l'addition`, tipping norms, `une terrasse`.

**Week 4 · The city & directions**
Function: ask and give directions, use the métro.
Grammar: `aller` + `au/à la/aux`; basic imperatives (`tournez, continuez`); prepositions of place.
Phonics: liaison after `aux`, `les`, numbers.
Vocab: métro/RER/RATP, arrondissements, landmarks, left/right/straight.
Paris note: reading a métro map; `correspondance`, `sortie`, `direction`.

**Week 5 · Time & daily routine**
Function: tell time, describe your day.
Grammar: reflexive/pronominal verbs (`se lever, se coucher`); `faire`; `être en train de`.
Phonics: rhythm and the phrase-final stress pattern.
Vocab: hours, days, months, routine verbs.
Paris note: 24-hour clock; opening hours and the `fermeture` culture (lunch, Sundays).

### Block C — Daily life (Weeks 6–8)

**Week 6 · Shopping & description**
Function: shop, describe objects, express quantity.
Grammar: demonstratives `ce/cet/cette/ces`; adjective agreement & placement (BAGS: beauty/age/goodness/size before the noun); `beaucoup de / un peu de`.
Phonics: adjective liaison (`un petit ami`).
Vocab: clothes, colors, sizes, quantities.
Paris note: `les soldes`, markets vs `supermarché`.

**Week 7 · Home & neighborhood**
Function: describe where you live.
Grammar: possessives `mon/ma/mes`…; regular `-ir` verbs (`finir`); more prepositions.
Phonics: `mon/ma` nasal contrast.
Vocab: rooms, furniture, the `quartier`.
Paris note: `un studio`, `au 3e étage`, `le gardien/la concierge`.

**Week 8 · Talking about the past (I)**
Function: say what you did.
Grammar: `passé composé` with `avoir`; common irregular past participles; time markers (`hier, la semaine dernière`).
Phonics: past participle endings that sound identical (`-é, -er, -ez`).
Vocab: weekend/activity verbs.
Paris note: recounting a weekend — small talk conventions.

### Block D — Expanding (Weeks 9–11)

**Week 9 · Talking about the past (II)**
Function: tell a short story.
Grammar: `passé composé` with `être` (the movement/change verbs) + agreement; `y` and `en` (light intro).
Phonics: agreement that's audible vs silent.
Vocab: travel/movement verbs.
Paris note: narrating a trip; sequencing with `d'abord, ensuite, après, enfin`.

**Week 10 · Plans & the near future**
Function: make plans, invite, refuse politely.
Grammar: `futur proche` (`aller` + infinitive); `on` for suggestions; weather.
Phonics: intonation of yes/no questions.
Vocab: leisure, invitations, weather.
Paris note: making and declining plans without bluntness; `ça te dit ?`

**Week 11 · Description & opinion**
Function: describe past states, compare, give an opinion.
Grammar: `imparfait` (intro — description & habit, contrasted lightly with `passé composé`); comparatives (`plus/moins… que`); opinion frames (`je trouve que, à mon avis`); `-re` verbs (`prendre, comprendre`).
Phonics: `imparfait` endings.
Vocab: opinion adjectives, comparison.
Paris note: café-debate register; softening disagreement.

### Block E — Consolidation (Week 12)

**Week 12 · Putting it together**
Function: hold a longer everyday exchange, switch register on purpose.
Grammar: integrated review — present / `passé composé` / `imparfait` / `futur proche` in one narrative.
Capstone: "Une journée à Paris" — a chained multi-scene dialogue (boulangerie → métro → museum → café → plans for tomorrow).
Assessment: self-check against A2 can-do statements (§6).

## 4. France-specific pitfalls baked into the curriculum

These are the things a Europe-default course gets subtly wrong or omits, and where "Parisian, not generic" earns its keep:

- **Numbers 70–99.** France: `soixante-dix (70)`, `quatre-vingts (80)`, `quatre-vingt-dix (90)`. (Belgian/Swiss `septante/nonante` are explicitly *excluded*.)
- **The bonjour ritual.** Entering a shop/bakery, `Bonjour` comes first, before your request. Skipping it reads as rude — taught Week 1.
- **`tu` vs `vous`.** Default `vous` with strangers/officials/shopkeepers; `tu` with peers/friends/children. Mis-`tu`-ing is a real social error, not a rounding difference.
- **`on` = "we".** Colloquial French uses `on` far more than `nous` for everyday "we". Introduced early so speech sounds natural.
- **`ne` dropping in speech.** In casual spoken French the `ne` of negation often disappears (`je sais pas`). Taught for comprehension, while writing keeps `ne`.
- **Liaison and elision.** Not decoration — they change what a beginner hears. Trained progressively from Week 2.
- **Register-appropriate slang.** Light, curated exposure to common Parisian informal words (e.g. `un truc, bosser, un mec`) — flagged as informal, never mixed into `vous` contexts.

## 5. Spaced repetition schedule

Each vocab item and key structure carries a `mastery` level 0–5. On a correct recall it advances; on a miss it drops one and re-queues sooner.

| Level | Next review after |
|-------|-------------------|
| 0 (new) | same session |
| 1 | +1 day |
| 2 | +3 days |
| 3 | +7 days |
| 4 | +16 days |
| 5 | +35 days (retired to occasional check) |

Review-day (day 7) draws the week's due items plus any lapsed ones from earlier weeks. This is a Leitner-style expanding-interval system — deliberately simple so it runs entirely on-device with no server.

---

## 6. Assessment — A2 can-do targets (end of Week 12)

The learner should be able to, in Parisian French:

- Greet appropriately and manage `tu`/`vous` without prompting.
- Order, shop, and handle prices and numbers to 100.
- Ask for and follow directions, including on the métro.
- Describe their routine, home, and neighborhood.
- Recount past events (`passé composé` + basic `imparfait`) and state near-future plans (`futur proche`).
- Give a simple opinion and a simple comparison.
- Follow slow, clear native-style speech on familiar topics.

## 7. Audio pipeline spec

- **Engine:** Azure Neural TTS, `fr-FR` voices. Suggested: **Denise** and **Vivienne** (female), **Henri** and **Remy** (male) for dialogue variety. (Canadian `fr-CA` voices exist but are *not* used — this is Parisian French.)
- **Generation:** pre-generate every clip once, key it by a stable `audioKey`, store as static files. Never live-TTS at runtime (worse latency, no offline, no consistency — the exact trap the "authentic vs robotic" claim should avoid).
- **The engine seam:** the player checks a clip map first and only falls back to browser TTS if a key is missing, so swapping the demo's synthetic voice for real pre-generated audio is a one-line change, not a rewrite.
- **Reality check:** Neural TTS is *less* robotic, not *authentic*. Informal Parisian prosody is still hard for TTS. Budget a native-speaker pass on the audio script (not just the text) to catch clips that land unnaturally.

---

## 8. The single biggest risk (don't skip this)

**Unverified content.** The entire pedagogical value rests on the generated French being correct — and a beginner, by definition, cannot catch the errors. Wrong genders, subtly unnatural phrasing, an "idiom" that's regional or dated, a grammar note that's 90% right. Before publishing, budget a **native-speaker / trained-teacher review pass** over the curriculum text *and* the audio script. This is the load-bearing wall of a language course, and it is the one thing AI generation alone cannot guarantee. Treat the generated 84 lessons as a first draft to be proofed, not a finished product.
